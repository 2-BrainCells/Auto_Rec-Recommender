3. Methodology

In this work, the primary goal was to evaluate the efficacy of neural network-based collaborative filtering, specifically an Autoencoder approach (AutoRec), in suggesting the optimal study tools and learning strategies to university students with dyslexia. Unlike traditional neighborhood-based methods (e.g., k-Nearest Neighbors), our approach seeks to learn complex, non-linear latent representations of student preferences. The methodology is structured into dataset preprocessing, model architecture design, training procedure, and evaluation protocol.

3.1. Dataset Preprocessing
The dataset utilized in this study derives from a questionnaire assessing the experiences of dyslexic students and the learning strategies or support tools they found useful. The initial dataset contains demographic features alongside user-item interaction scores. However, to ensure that the recommendations are strictly behavior-driven and free from demographic-induced bias, the first 12 columns corresponding to demographic information were systematically excluded. 

The qualitative responses gathered from the questionnaire were initially transformed into a numerical scale ranging from 0 to 5. Responses indicating uncertain or unexplored interactions (e.g., 'NC', 'NSU', or blank entries) were mapped to 'NaN' (Not a Number) to represent unobserved interactions. Given the inherent sparsity of recommendation matrices, data quality control was enforced by calculating the missing-value ratio per user. Students lacking interactions for more than 40% of the item space were excluded from the study. 

Finally, the valid interaction scores were normalized by a scaling factor of 5.0. This transformation projects the rating values into a [0, 1] continuous space, stabilizing gradients and facilitating efficient convergence during Neural Network training. The final preprocessed data was structured as a sparse user-item interaction matrix.

3.2. Algorithm and Architecture (AutoRec)
To capture the implicit collaborative signals among students, we implemented an item-based Autoencoder model. AutoRec maps a partially observed user interaction vector $r^{(u)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (where $d$ represents the total number of items) into a low-dimensional latent space, and subsequently reconstructs the scores for all items to formulate recommendations.

The architecture comprises:
- An Encoder layer that applies a linear transformation followed by a non-linear Sigmoid activation function to compress the sparse input vector into a dense feature representation.
- A Regularization phase applying a Dropout rate of $p = 0.2$ to the hidden representations to prevent overfitting on the sparse dataset.
- A Decoder layer consisting of a linear transformation that expands the latent representation back to the original $d$-dimensional space, outputting the predicted rating vector $\hat{r}^{(u)}$.

3.3. Training Procedure and Masked Loss
A critical challenge in training autoencoders for recommendation systems is the predominance of unobserved ratings. Treating missing values as true negative observations (zeroes) fundamentally distorts the learning objective. To circumvent this, the model was trained using a custom Masked Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function.

During the forward propagation, a binary mask matrix was dynamically generated to filter the outputs. The backpropagation and gradient updates were computed strictly over the set of observed items, defined as:
$MaskedLoss = \frac{\sum (Mask \cdot (\hat{r} - r)^2)}{\sum Mask + \epsilon}$
The model parameters were optimized using the Adam optimizer. To optimize strictly for ranking fidelity, an early stopping criterion was adopted: the training loop monitored the Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) on a validation split, halting training if the ranking metric showed no improvement over a predefined patience threshold.

3.4. Experimental Setup and Assessment
To validate the model’s robustness and prevent data leakage, an extensive 5-Fold Cross-Validation framework was utilized. To isolate and confirm the effectiveness of the proposed architectural decisions, an ablation study was programmed comparing:
1. The Proposed AutoRec model.
2. AutoRec without Dropout regularization.
3. A baseline employing a dense MSE Loss (without masking).

During the inference phase, items present in the training interactions were masked out to prevent the trivial retrieval of already-known items. The system then ranked the remaining unrated items to formulate the Top-K recommendations.

4. Results

This section presents the empirical outcomes of the proposed AutoRec architecture. The performance of the system was evaluated not merely on point-wise predictive errors but comprehensively across ranking quality, fairness, and item distribution profiles.

4.1. Overall Predictive Accuracy
Before evaluating the system's ability to rank items accurately, its capacity to estimate true preference scores was analyzed. The global Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) achieved by the AutoRec model was remarkably stable at 0.3480 across all folds. This score confirms that the implementation of the masked MSE loss successfully guided the autoencoder to accurately reconstruct observed ratings without being disproportionately penalized by the high sparsity of the dyslexic student matrix. 

4.2. Top-K Recommendation Quality
To evaluate the true-world utility of the system, we assessed its performance using precision-based ranking metrics, specifically Recall@K and NDCG@K, across three varying recommendation windows: K=3, K=5, and K=10 (see Table 1).

Table 1: Multi-K Evaluation Summary
| Metric | K=3 | K=5 | K=10 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Recall@K | 0.4696 | 0.6799 | 0.9343 |
| NDCG@K | 0.4954 | 0.5755 | 0.6865 |
| Diversity | 0.2784 | 0.3017 | 0.3595 |
| Novelty | 5.1274 | 5.1712 | 5.2962 |
| Coverage | 0.9487 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| RMSE (Global) | 0.3480 | 0.3480 | 0.3480 |

The data confirms a strong operational capability in prioritizing relevant support methodologies for dyslexic students. Even at the highly constrained K=3 boundary, the system retrieves roughly 47% of relevant items with an NDCG of 0.4954. As the threshold expands to K=10, the Recall climbs to 0.9343, indicating that over 93% of the most beneficial items are successfully retrieved within the top 10 positions. Concurrently, the NDCG@10 of 0.6865 demonstrates that the AutoRec effectively sorts the items, ensuring the most vital strategies appear at the top of the recommended list.

4.3. Catalog Coverage and Long-tail Discovery
Traditional Nearest-neighbor recommendation algorithms often suffer from popularity bias, isolating users to a limited pool of generic items. This is particularly detrimental in educational support, where specialized, niche tools can provide immense personalized value to dyslexic students.

Our framework systematically overcame this limitation. The Coverage metric illustrates the percentage of the catalog deployed to users; impressively, at K=3, the system explored 94.87% of the total dataset space. At K=5 and K=10, the Coverage reached an absolute 1.0000 (100%), signaling that every item in the dataset was successfully recommended to at least one student who needed it. 

Furthermore, the continuous rise in the Novelty score (from 5.1274 at K=3 to 5.2962 at K=10) indicates the system's proficiency in delving into the "long-tail" of the dataset, successfully suggesting lesser-known but highly relevant tools. Coupled with a Diversity metric scaling to 0.3595, these results prove that the AutoRec architecture is a highly diverse, fair, and precise pipeline for generating cognitive support recommendations.
